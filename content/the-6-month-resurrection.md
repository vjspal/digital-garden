## The Legacy Institution Problem

Resurrection Catholic Missions (RCM) is a historic Black Catholic institution in Montgomery, Alabama, standing at the intersection of food security, education, and racial justice. But institutions cannot run on mission alone. When I arrived, the modern tools were not just lagging; they were broken. The website was a mobile disaster, communication was fragmented across personal accounts, and school enrollment had hit an all-time low.

The stakes were financial, not just aesthetic. Alabama's CHOOSE Act offers up to $7,000 per student in education savings account funding — a real opportunity RCM was at risk of losing if parents took those funds to schools with better digital front doors. I returned to Montgomery as an alumnus who had spent years in Japan, bringing an outsider's "digital strategist" lens back to the parish of my childhood. I didn't come to "update a website." I came to deliver a high-level digital roadmap to help ensure the mission's survival.

## The "One Number" Rule: Why Your Mobile Bounce Rate Is the Metric of Truth

In digital transformation, the temptation is to start with "the look" — new logos, fresh colors, a "modern" vibe. That's usually a distraction. For RCM, the original pain point was mobile rendering. If a parishioner or donor can't navigate your site on a five-year-old Android, your mission loses trust before anyone reads a single word.

We began with an un-building. We didn't add features first; we purged technical debt — deactivating heavy, unused plugins to claw back mobile performance. I prioritized mobile bounce rate as our metric of truth: legible, measurable, and directly tied to technical health. The discipline was simple — if you can't measure it, it didn't happen.

"Baseline capture is Phase 1, and it is not skippable — miss it and the comparison is lost permanently."

## Owning the Audience: Why Social Media Is "Rented" and Email Is "Owned"

Most organizations treat social media as their primary outreach strategy. Strategically, that's a mistake. Social media is a rented audience — you're a tenant on a platform whose algorithm can evict your content at any moment.

The RCM roadmap shifted focus toward a CRM and an owned email list. To populate it, we built "lead magnets" — high-value content offered in exchange for a direct connection. This wasn't generic "church content." We used specific, localized assets: a parish cookbook for donors, and community fitness videos for the youth. That infrastructure is what actually converts passive scrolling into the donations and grant credibility an institution needs to survive.

## Redesigning Around Trust, Not Just Tools

A recurring theme of this project was that infrastructure decisions are really governance decisions. Who owns an account, who has admin access, and how content moves from a person's private feed into an institution's official channel — all of that determines whether a digital presence survives any one person's departure. The fix, again and again, wasn't to take control away from the people already doing the work by hand. It was to hand them a better, shared tool and make the official channel the easier path.

## The Manual Soak: The Counter-Intuitive Case Against Early Automation

The modern impulse is to automate everything on day one. That's how you automate the wrong thing. Our roadmap called for a six-week "manual soak" period during the high-traffic months of November and December.

We ran three distinct tracks by hand: Facebook, primarily for elders and longtime donors; Instagram, for working parents and younger adults; and TikTok/Reels, for youth and enrollment-age families.

By doing the work manually — repurposing Father Manuel's homilies into different formats for each platform — we identified the real friction points before we automated anything. The manual soak taught us what actually resonated with parishioners before we spent a dime on tooling.

## AI With a Conscience: The Ethics of Voice Cloning

We explored cloning Father Manuel's distinctive, recognizable voice to narrate older institutional writings. Technically, it was easy. Morally, we hit a wall. In a religious context, trust is the only currency that matters.

The decision was deferred on the principle of disclosure vs. deception:

"A synthetic version passing as real would feel dishonest to parishioners and could damage trust badly."

If we ever deploy synthetic voice, it will carry an explicit disclosure — narrated using Father's voice, with permission. Integrity in digital transformation means never letting the tool outpace the ethics of the institution.

## Success Is an Exit Strategy: The 6-Month Handoff

In high-level consulting, success isn't a "better-looking site." It's a system that survives the consultant. At RCM, the goal was for the parish to run its own communications without me by the six-month mark.

That meant a train-the-teacher program and course-quality video tutorials. But the real strategic win was the STEM/media skills initiative — not just training staff, but designing a program where students produce content as part of their curriculum. That turned a technical necessity into a grant-worthy educational asset. If the system dies three months after handoff, the project failed regardless of what the metrics said.

## Conclusion: The Power of the Build Log

The discipline anchoring this entire transformation was a raw build log — not a polished report, but a timestamped capture of every decision, dead end, and technical "un-building." The operating principle was simple: redact at publish time, not capture time.

By capturing everything as it happened, we created a repository of institutional knowledge that keeps future leaders from relitigating past decisions.

As you look at your own organization, ask yourself: if you left tomorrow, would your digital systems survive your departure — or are you the only thing keeping the lights on? Real digital transformation is about the systems you leave behind, not the tools you buy.
